\section{Three stochastic epidemic models}
\label{sec:models}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

We consider three discrete-time, branching-process models for the spread of
an infectious disease.  Time is measured in days.  Let $I_t \geq 0$ denote
the number of new infections (incidence) on day $t$, and let $R_t > 0$ be
the effective reproduction number on day $t$.

The \emph{serial interval weights} $w_s > 0$ ($s = 1, 2, \ldots$) satisfy
$\sum_{s=1}^\infty w_s = 1$ and encode the probability that a secondary
infection appears $s$ days after the primary case.  Write
\[
  F_r = \sum_{s=1}^r w_s
\]
for the cumulative serial interval distribution at lag $r$, so
$1 - F_r = \sum_{s=r+1}^\infty w_s$ is the probability that transmission
occurs after day $r$.

The first two models share a \emph{dispersion parameter} $k > 0$
controlling individual-level variation in infectiousness.  Small $k$
corresponds to high heterogeneity (superspreading); as $k \to \infty$
both models collapse onto a common Poisson offspring distribution with no
dispersion parameter — our third model.

\begin{remark}
  Throughout, $\mathrm{NB}(\text{mean} = \mu, \text{disp} = k)$ denotes the
  negative-binomial distribution with probability mass function
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(X = n)
    = \binom{n + k - 1}{n}
    \left(\frac{k}{k + \mu}\right)^{\!k}
    \left(\frac{\mu}{k + \mu}\right)^{\!n},
    \quad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots,
  \]
  mean $\mu$, variance $\mu + \mu^2/k$, and probability generating function
  (pgf) $G(s) = \bigl(k\,/\,(k + \mu(1-s))\bigr)^k$.
  Similarly, $\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ denotes the Gamma distribution
  with shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$, so density
  $f(x) = \beta^\alpha x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x}/\Gamma(\alpha)$,
  mean $\alpha/\beta$, and variance $\alpha/\beta^2$.
\end{remark}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{SSE model (Superspreading Events)}
\label{sec:sse}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

In the \emph{superspreading-events} (SSE) model, the force of infection on
day $t$ is the weighted sum of past incidence,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_t = \sum_{s=1}^{\infty} w_s\, I_{t-s},
\end{equation}
and new infections are negative-binomially distributed:
\begin{equation}
  I_t \mid \lambda_t \;\sim\;
  \mathrm{NB}\!\left(\text{mean} = R_t \lambda_t,\;\text{disp} =
  k\lambda_t\right).
\end{equation}
The conditional mean of $I_t$ is $R_t \lambda_t$ and its variance is
$R_t \lambda_t + R_t^2 \lambda_t / k$, so the NB inflates the Poisson
variance by the factor $1 + R_t \lambda_t / k$, capturing superspreading at
the population level.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{SSI model (Superspreading Individuals)}
\label{sec:ssi}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

In the \emph{superspreading individuals} (SSI) model, heterogeneity
enters at the individual level.  Each infected individual has a
latent infectivity that is Gamma distributed with mean~1 and variance~$1/k$.

Each cohort of $I_t$ infected individuals then has an aggregate infectivity
\begin{equation}
  Y_t \mid I_t \;\sim\;
  \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(\text{shape} = k I_t,\;\text{rate} = k\right),
\end{equation}
so $\mathbb{E}[Y_t \mid I_t] = I_t$ and
$\mathrm{Var}(Y_t \mid I_t) = I_t/k$.  New infections arise as a Poisson
process driven by past infectivity,
\begin{equation}
  I_t \mid (Y_s)_{s < t} \;\sim\;
  \mathrm{Poisson}\!\left(R_t \sum_{s=1}^{\infty} w_s\, Y_{t-s}\right).
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}
  Marginalising $Y_t$ from the SSI model recovers a negative-binomial
  offspring distribution (by the Poisson--Gamma mixture identity; see
  below), so the two models share the same mean--variance structure at the
  population level while differing in the \emph{mechanism} generating
  overdispersion.
\end{remark}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Poisson offspring model}
\label{sec:poisson}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

In the \emph{Poisson} offspring model the force of infection on day $t$ is
the same weighted sum as in the SSE model,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_t = \sum_{s=1}^{\infty} w_s\, I_{t-s},
\end{equation}
and new infections are Poisson distributed:
\begin{equation}
  I_t \mid \lambda_t \;\sim\;
  \mathrm{Poisson}\!\left(R_t \lambda_t\right).
\end{equation}
The conditional mean and variance of $I_t$ are both $R_t \lambda_t$:
the Poisson is the limit of the SSE-style overdispersion vanishing
($1 + R_t \lambda_t / k \to 1$ as $k \to \infty$).

\begin{remark}
  The Poisson model is the common $k \to \infty$ limit of both the SSE
  and the SSI models.  In the SSE step the negative-binomial offspring
  distribution $\mathrm{NB}(R_t \lambda_t, k\lambda_t)$ collapses to
  $\mathrm{Poisson}(R_t \lambda_t)$ as $k \to \infty$; in the SSI step
  the Gamma noise on the individual infectivities degenerates to
  $Y_t = I_t$ a.s., again leaving $\mathrm{Poisson}(R_t \lambda_t)$ for
  the increment.  There is no dispersion parameter; the only model
  parameter is $R_t$.
\end{remark}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Extinction probability}
\label{sec:extinction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Henceforth assume the reproduction number is constant, $R_t = R_0$.  We
study the probability that the epidemic goes extinct, starting from a single
infectious case on day~0.

By branching-process theory, the extinction probability $q$ is the smallest
non-negative fixed point of the pgf of the offspring distribution.  For the
SSE and SSI models the offspring distribution is
$\mathrm{NB}(\text{mean} = R_0, \text{disp} = k)$, whose pgf is
$G(s) = (k/(k + R_0(1-s)))^k$; setting $q = G(q)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q_nb}
  q = \left(1 + \frac{R_0}{k}(1 - q)\right)^{-k}.
\end{equation}
For the Poisson model the offspring distribution is
$\mathrm{Poisson}(R_0)$, whose pgf is $G(s) = e^{R_0(s - 1)}$; setting
$q = G(q)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q_poisson}
  q = e^{R_0(q - 1)}.
\end{equation}
In all three models, if $R_0 \leq 1$ the unique solution is $q = 1$
(extinction is certain); if $R_0 > 1$ there is a unique solution
$q \in (0,1)$, so the probability of a major outbreak is $1 - q > 0$.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Extinction probability after observing incidence on days 0 to \(r\)}
\label{sec:qr}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose we observe incidence $I_0, I_1, \ldots, I_r$ on the first $r+1$
days, with $I_0 \geq 1$.  We compute the updated extinction probability
$q_r$ for the residual epidemic — the probability that the chain triggered
by the observed cases eventually dies out.

A standard branching-process argument applies in all three models: each
remaining offspring of the observed cases, once born, initiates an
independent fresh epidemic governed by the original offspring
distribution (NB($R_0$, $k$) for SSE/SSI, $\mathrm{Poisson}(R_0)$ for
Poisson) and goes extinct with the standard probability $q$ (the
smallest fixed point of $G$).  The conditioning event constrains only
the offspring of the observed cases on days $0, \ldots, r$; it does not
propagate to later generations.  Therefore
\[
  q_r = G_r(q),
\]
where $G_r$ is the pgf of the (conditional) total remaining offspring of
the observed cases.  The three models differ in the form of $G_r$.

% ---- SSE ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{SSE model}
\label{sec:qr_sse}

In the SSE model the offspring process decomposes into independent
contributions: each of the $I_s$ individuals infected on day $s$ generates
a number of offspring at lag $\ell$ that is
$\mathrm{NB}(\text{mean} = R_0 w_\ell,\;\text{disp} = k w_\ell)$,
independently across individuals, donor days, and lags.  By the NB closure
property under a common success probability
$p = k w_\ell / (k w_\ell + R_0 w_\ell) = k / (k + R_0)$ (independent of
$\ell$), the population-level $\mathrm{NB}(R_0 \lambda_t, k \lambda_t)$ law
of $I_t$ is recovered by summing all per-individual-per-lag contributions.

Conditioning on the observed counts $I_0, \ldots, I_r$ pins down only those
contributions $(s, \ell)$ with $s + \ell \leq r$.  Per-individual-per-lag
contributions with $s \in \{0, \ldots, r\}$ and $\ell > r - s$ are
independent of the observations and retain their original distributions.
The total remaining offspring of the observed cases is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{s=0}^{r} \sum_{i=1}^{I_s} \sum_{\ell = r-s+1}^{\infty}
  \mathrm{NB}(\text{mean} = R_0 w_\ell,\;\text{disp} = k w_\ell)
  = \mathrm{NB}(\text{mean} = R_0 \Lambda,\;\text{disp} = k \Lambda),
\end{equation}
where the pooled effective weight is
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda \;=\; \sum_{s=0}^{r} I_s\,(1 - F_{r-s}).
\end{equation}
With the convention $F_0 = 0$, the $s = r$ term enters with full weight,
since none of its offspring have had a chance to appear yet.

The pgf is
$G_r(s) = \bigl(1 + (R_0/k)(1-s)\bigr)^{-k\Lambda} = G(s)^{\Lambda}$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qr_sse}
  q_r \;=\; G_r(q) \;=\; q^{\Lambda}
  \;=\; q^{\,\sum_{s=0}^{r} I_s\,(1 - F_{r-s})}.
\end{equation}
The earlier special case (a single case on day~0 followed by $r$ days
without cases) corresponds to $I_0 = 1$, $I_1 = \cdots = I_r = 0$, giving
$\Lambda = 1 - F_r$ and recovering $q_r = q^{1 - F_r}$.  In the same
direction, as the zero streak extends ($r \to \infty$) the contributions
$I_s\,(1-F_{r-s})$ all vanish and $q_r \to 1$: extinction approaches
certainty.

% ---- SSI ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{SSI model}
\label{sec:qr_ssi}

For the SSI model the joint posterior of $(Y_0,\ldots,Y_{r-1})$ is in
general not available in closed form: as soon as $I_d > 0$ on at least
one day $d \in \{1, \ldots, r\}$, the day-$d$ rate
$R_0 \sum_{s=1}^{d-1} w_{d-s} Y_s$ couples the latents.  We therefore
present an MCMC estimator of $q_r$ here as the canonical SSI workflow.
For the special cases of cases on day~$0$ only, on day~$0$ and one
later day, and on day~$0$ and two later days, closed forms are however
available; these are deferred to \S~\ref{sec:ssi_closed_forms} below.

\paragraph{MCMC posterior.}

We sample $(Y_0,\ldots,Y_{r-1})$ from their joint posterior given
$(I_0,\ldots,I_r)$ using a Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampler (implemented in
PyMC).  Note that $Y_r$, the aggregate infectivity of the day-$r$ cohort,
has not yet contributed to any observed outcome; its posterior equals its
prior,
\[
  Y_r \;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}(kI_r,\, k),
\]
and is sampled independently.

\paragraph{Remaining offspring from observed cases.}

Given $(Y_s)_{s=0}^r$, the future infections generated by the observed cases
arrive as independent Poisson streams: cohort $s$ contributes at lag
$\ell \geq r - s + 1$ at rate $R_0 w_\ell Y_s$.  Summing over cohorts and
lags gives total remaining offspring
\begin{equation}
  I_{\mathrm{rem}} \mid (Y_s)_{s=0}^r
  \;\sim\;
  \mathrm{Poisson}\!\left(R_0\,\Lambda\right),
  \qquad
  \Lambda \;=\; \sum_{s=0}^r Y_s\,(1-F_{r-s}),
\end{equation}
exactly the same pooled-weight formula as in the SSE model.

\paragraph{Extinction probability and MCMC estimator.}

Each remaining offspring initiates an independent fresh epidemic that goes
extinct with the standard probability $q$.  Hence the conditional extinction
probability of the residual epidemic, given the latent infectivities, is the
pgf of $\mathrm{Poisson}(R_0\Lambda)$ evaluated at $q$:
\begin{equation}
  q_r \mid (Y_s)_{s=0}^r \;=\; e^{R_0\,\Lambda\,(q-1)}.
\end{equation}
Marginalising over the posterior of $(Y_s)$ and estimating by a Monte Carlo
average over $M$ MCMC draws,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q}_r \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} e^{R_0\,\Lambda^{(m)}\,(q-1)},
  \qquad
  \widehat{\mathrm{PMO}} \;=\; 1 - \hat{q}_r,
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda^{(m)} = \sum_{s=0}^r Y_s^{(m)}(1-F_{r-s})$.

\begin{remark}[Consistency with the day-0-only analytic formula]
  When $I_1 = \cdots = I_r = 0$ the observations force $Y_1 = \cdots = Y_r =
  0$, so $\Lambda = Y_0(1-F_r)$ and the posterior is
  $Y_0 \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(kI_0,\,k+R_0 F_r)$.  Taking the expectation of
  $e^{R_0 Y_0(1-F_r)(q-1)}$ with respect to this posterior via the Gamma
  moment-generating function gives
  \[
    q_r
    = \left(1 - \frac{R_0(1-F_r)(q-1)}{k+R_0 F_r}\right)^{-kI_0}
    = \left(1 + \frac{R_0(1-F_r)}{k+R_0 F_r}(1-q)\right)^{-kI_0},
  \]
  which matches the closed-form result \eqref{eq:qr_case_i}.
\end{remark}

% ---- Poisson --------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Poisson model}
\label{sec:qr_poisson}

The argument of \S~\ref{sec:qr_sse} carries over verbatim with NB
replaced by Poisson.  Each of the $I_s$ individuals infected on day $s$
generates a $\mathrm{Poisson}(R_0 w_\ell)$ number of offspring at lag
$\ell$, independently across individuals, donor days, and lags.  By
Poisson closure under summation the total remaining offspring of the
observed cases is
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{s=0}^{r} \sum_{i=1}^{I_s} \sum_{\ell = r-s+1}^{\infty}
  \mathrm{Poisson}(R_0 w_\ell)
  \;=\;
  \mathrm{Poisson}\!\left(R_0\,\Lambda\right),
\end{equation}
with the same pooled effective weight
$\Lambda = \sum_{s=0}^{r} I_s\,(1 - F_{r-s})$ as in the SSE model.  The
pgf is $G_r(s) = e^{R_0\Lambda(s-1)} = G(s)^{\Lambda}$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qr_poisson}
  q_r \;=\; G_r(q) \;=\; q^{\Lambda},
\end{equation}
identical in functional form to \eqref{eq:qr_sse} but with $q$ the
Poisson extinction root \eqref{eq:q_poisson}.  In particular, both
\eqref{eq:qr_sse} and \eqref{eq:qr_poisson} are closed-form for any
observed history.

\section{Probability of major outbreak under model uncertainty}
\label{sec:pmo_uncertain}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

So far we have computed the probability of a major outbreak conditional on
a chosen model (SSE, SSI, or Poisson).  In practice the underlying
mechanism generating overdispersion is unknown, and we may wish to combine
the candidate models via Bayesian model averaging.  Let $\mathcal{M}$
denote a finite set of candidate models with prior probabilities
$\{\pi_M\}_{M \in \mathcal{M}}$ satisfying
$\sum_{M \in \mathcal{M}} \pi_M = 1$.  Two natural choices are the
two-model set $\mathcal{M} = \{\mathrm{SSE}, \mathrm{SSI}\}$ (a single
$k$, varying only the noise mechanism) and a wider ensemble including
several SSE and SSI specifications with different $k$ values together
with the Poisson limit.  Given the observed history $(I_0, \ldots, I_r)$,
Bayes' theorem yields posterior model probabilities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pi_post_general}
  \pi_M^\star
  \;=\; \frac{\pi_M\, L_M}
  {\sum_{M' \in \mathcal{M}} \pi_{M'}\, L_{M'}},
  \qquad
  L_M \;=\; \mathbb{P}_M(I_1, \ldots, I_r \mid I_0),
\end{equation}
and the model-averaged probability of a major outbreak is the posterior
expectation of the per-model PMOs,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pmo_avg_general}
  \mathrm{PMO}_\mathrm{avg}
  \;=\; \sum_{M \in \mathcal{M}} \pi_M^\star\,\mathrm{PMO}_M,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{PMO}_M = 1 - q_r^M$ as derived above.  Specialising to the
two-model case $\mathcal{M} = \{\mathrm{SSE}, \mathrm{SSI}\}$,
\eqref{eq:pi_post_general} and \eqref{eq:pmo_avg_general} become the
familiar pair
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pmo_avg_two_model}
  \pi_\mathrm{SSE}^\star
  \;=\; \frac{\pi_\mathrm{SSE}\, L_\mathrm{SSE}}
  {\pi_\mathrm{SSE}\, L_\mathrm{SSE} + \pi_\mathrm{SSI}\, L_\mathrm{SSI}},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{PMO}_\mathrm{avg}
  \;=\; \pi_\mathrm{SSE}^\star\,\mathrm{PMO}_\mathrm{SSE}
  + \pi_\mathrm{SSI}^\star\,\mathrm{PMO}_\mathrm{SSI}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Likelihood of the observed history}
\label{sec:likelihood}

\paragraph{SSE model.}  Conditional on past incidence, the day-$t$ count
satisfies $I_t \mid I_{<t} \sim \mathrm{NB}(\text{mean} = R_0 \lambda_t,
\text{disp} = k \lambda_t)$ with
$\lambda_t = \sum_{s=1}^{t-1} w_{t-s} I_s$, so the joint likelihood
factorises into a product of negative-binomial pmfs,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_sse_general}
  L_\mathrm{SSE}(I_1, \ldots, I_r \mid I_0)
  \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^r \mathrm{NB}\!\bigl(I_t;\, R_0 \lambda_t,\,
  k\lambda_t\bigr).
\end{equation}
This closed form is available for any history.

\paragraph{Poisson model.}  Likewise, the day-$t$ count satisfies
$I_t \mid I_{<t} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(R_0 \lambda_t)$, so the joint
likelihood factorises into a product of Poisson pmfs,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_poisson_general}
  L_\mathrm{Poisson}(I_1, \ldots, I_r \mid I_0)
  \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^r \mathrm{Poisson}\!\bigl(I_t;\, R_0 \lambda_t\bigr),
\end{equation}
closed-form for any history.  As with \eqref{eq:L_sse_general}, the
convention is $\log L = -\infty$ whenever $\lambda_t = 0$ but $I_t > 0$.

\paragraph{SSI model, closed-form cases.}  Closed forms are available in the
same three special cases as for $q_r$ (cases on day~$0$ only, on day~$0$ and
one later day, on day~$0$ and two later days); these are derived in
\S~\ref{sec:ssi_closed_forms} below.

\paragraph{SSI model, general histories.}  The likelihood involves the
latent infectivities $(Y_s)_{s=0}^{r-1}$ and is in general not available in
closed form.  Unfolding the SSI generative dependence
$I_0 \to Y_0 \to I_1 \to Y_1 \to \cdots \to I_r$ as a Markov chain, the
joint density of latents and observations factorises as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint_ssi}
  p(Y_{0:r-1},\, I_{1:r} \mid I_0)
  \;=\; \prod_{t=0}^{r-1} p_Y(Y_t \mid I_t)
  \,\cdot\,
  \prod_{t=1}^{r} p_I\!\bigl(I_t \mid Y_{0:t-1}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $p_Y(\,\cdot\, \mid I_t) = \mathrm{Gamma}(\cdot;\, kI_t,\, k)$ is the
SSI generative density of $Y_t$ given the cohort size on day~$t$, and
$p_I(\,\cdot\, \mid Y_{0:t-1}) = \mathrm{Poisson}(\cdot;\, R_0
\sum_{s=1}^{t} w_s Y_{t-s})$.  Marginalising the latents,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_ssi_integral}
  L_\mathrm{SSI}(I_{1:r} \mid I_0)
  \;=\; \int \!\prod_{t=0}^{r-1} p_Y(Y_t \mid I_t)
  \,\prod_{t=1}^{r} p_I(I_t \mid Y_{0:t-1})\, \mathrm{d}Y_{0:r-1}.
\end{equation}
Days $s$ with $I_s = 0$ collapse $Y_s$ to a point mass at $0$, and $Y_r$
does not enter $L_\mathrm{SSI}$ (it would only contribute to $I_{r+1},
I_{r+2}, \ldots$), so the integration runs effectively over
$\mathcal{T} = \{t \in \{0,\ldots,r-1\} : I_t > 0\}$.  Three MCMC-based
estimators of \eqref{eq:L_ssi_integral} are derived in
\S~\ref{sec:mcmc_evidence}.

\subsection{Simulation-based estimator}
\label{sec:simulation}

For general histories, where the SSI likelihood is unavailable in closed
form, we use a rejection-sampling estimator that simultaneously realises
the posterior model weighting.  We describe it for
$\mathcal{M} = \{\mathrm{SSE}, \mathrm{SSI}\}$; it extends to any finite
$\mathcal{M}$ by replacing the Binomial split below with a Multinomial
split by prior, and the acceptance-rate argument carries over unchanged.
Repeat the following in batches of size $b$:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Draw the per-batch model split as a Binomial:
    $b_\mathrm{SSE} \sim \mathrm{Bin}(b, \pi_\mathrm{SSE})$ and
    $b_\mathrm{SSI} = b - b_\mathrm{SSE}$.
  \item Run $b_\mathrm{SSE}$ vectorised SSE forward simulations from $t=0$
    seeded with $I_0$ index cases, retaining only those whose simulated
    incidence on days $1, \ldots, r$ exactly matches the observed
    $I_1, \ldots, I_r$.  Likewise for $b_\mathrm{SSI}$ SSI simulations.
  \item For each retained simulation, continue forward until either the
    single-step incidence reaches the major-outbreak threshold (resolved as
    ``major'') or the most recent $L = \mathrm{len}(w)$ steps are all zero
    (resolved as ``extinct'').
\end{enumerate}

Continue accumulating batches until a target number of resolved-and-matching
simulations is reached.  The acceptance rate of model $M$'s simulations is
$L_M$ in expectation; with a Binomial split by prior, the expected number
of accepted $M$-sims is proportional to $\pi_M L_M$, so the composition of
the accepted pool matches the posterior $\pi_M^\star$.  The pooled estimator
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{PMO}}_\mathrm{avg}
  \;=\; \frac{N_\mathrm{SSE}^{\mathrm{maj}} + N_\mathrm{SSI}^{\mathrm{maj}}}
  {N_\mathrm{SSE}^{\mathrm{acc}} + N_\mathrm{SSI}^{\mathrm{acc}}}
  \;=\; \widehat{\pi}_\mathrm{SSE}^\star\,\widehat{\mathrm{PMO}}_\mathrm{SSE}
  + \widehat{\pi}_\mathrm{SSI}^\star\,\widehat{\mathrm{PMO}}_\mathrm{SSI}
\end{equation}
follows immediately, where $\widehat{\pi}_M^\star = N_M^{\mathrm{acc}} /
(N_\mathrm{SSE}^{\mathrm{acc}} + N_\mathrm{SSI}^{\mathrm{acc}})$ is the
implied posterior estimate and $\widehat{\mathrm{PMO}}_M = N_M^{\mathrm{maj}}
/ N_M^{\mathrm{acc}}$ is the per-model PMO from the accepted $M$-sims.

\section{SSI model: closed forms for histories with cases on a few days}
\label{sec:ssi_closed_forms}
% ===========================================================================

For the SSI model, when non-zero incidence is observed on day~$0$ and
on a small number of later days, the joint posterior of the latent
infectivities still factorises (or factorises as a finite mixture) and
both the model evidence $L_\mathrm{SSI}$ and the residual extinction
probability $q_r$ admit closed forms.  This section treats three such
cases in turn: case~(i) (\S~\ref{sec:ssi_case_i}), incidence on day~$0$
only; case~(ii) (\S~\ref{sec:ssi_case_ii}), incidence on day~$0$ and one
later day; and case~(iii) (\S~\ref{sec:ssi_case_iii}), incidence on day~$0$
and two later days.

\paragraph{Strategy.}  Each derivation follows the same five steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Conditional likelihood given the latents.}  Multiply the
    per-day Poisson pmfs to obtain
    $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{obs} \mid Y_0, Y_{i_1}, \ldots, Y_{i_n})$.
  \item \emph{Joint density of latents and observation.}  Multiply by
    the independent Gamma priors $Y_s \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(k I_s, k)$ to
    get $p(Y_0, Y_{i_1}, \ldots, Y_{i_n}, \mathrm{obs})$, with all
    normalising constants visible.
  \item \emph{Likelihood (model evidence).}  Integrate the joint density
    over the latents using the Gamma normalisation
    $\int_0^\infty Y^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta Y}\,\mathrm{d}Y =
    \Gamma(\alpha)/\beta^{\alpha}$ in each variable.
  \item \emph{Posterior of the latents.}  Divide the joint density by
    the likelihood to read off the posterior; in the cases below this
    yields independent Gammas (or a finite mixture of independent-Gamma
    products in case~(iii)).
  \item \emph{Residual extinction probability and PMO.}  The conditional
    extinction probability given the latents is
    $q_r \mid (Y_s) = e^{-R_0(1-q)\Lambda}$ with
    $\Lambda = \sum_{s=0}^r Y_s(1 - F_{r-s})$ (cf.\ the SSI MCMC
    derivation in \S~SSI model above); marginalising over the posterior
    via the Gamma MGF
    $\mathbb{E}[e^{-tX}] = (1 + t/\beta)^{-\alpha}$ for
    $X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ gives a closed-form $q_r$,
    and the probability of a major outbreak is $\mathrm{PMO} = 1 - q_r$.
\end{enumerate}

A key identity used throughout is that $I_s = 0$ on day~$s$ forces
$Y_s = 0$ (since $Y_s \mid I_s \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(k I_s, k)$ collapses
to a point mass at $0$), so among the latents only those tied to
non-zero observed days are non-degenerate.  Likewise, the SSE
likelihood \eqref{eq:L_sse_general} is closed-form for any history,
so each subsection below ends with the model-averaged PMO via Bayes'
theorem from the per-model likelihoods and PMOs.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Case (i): \(I_0 \geq 1\), \(I_1 = \cdots = I_r = 0\)}
\label{sec:ssi_case_i}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Only $Y_0$ is non-degenerate; we abbreviate $y = Y_0$.

\paragraph{Step 1: conditional likelihood.}
Given $y$, $I_d$ is independently $\mathrm{Poisson}(R_0 w_d y)$ for
$d = 1, \ldots, r$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cl_case_i}
  \mathbb{P}(I_1 = \cdots = I_r = 0 \mid y)
  \;=\; \prod_{d=1}^r e^{-R_0 w_d y}
  \;=\; e^{-R_0 F_r y}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 2: joint density.}
Multiplying \eqref{eq:cl_case_i} by the prior
$y \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(k I_0, k)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jd_case_i}
  p(y, \mathrm{obs})
  \;=\; \frac{k^{k I_0}}{\Gamma(k I_0)}\,
  y^{k I_0 - 1}\, e^{-(k + R_0 F_r) y}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 3: likelihood.}
Integrating $y$ out of \eqref{eq:jd_case_i} via the Gamma normalisation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_ssi_case_i}
  L_\mathrm{SSI}^{(\mathrm{i})}
  \;=\; \frac{k^{k I_0}}{\Gamma(k I_0)}\cdot
  \frac{\Gamma(k I_0)}{(k + R_0 F_r)^{k I_0}}
  \;=\; \left(1 + \frac{R_0 F_r}{k}\right)^{-k I_0}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 4: posterior.}
Dividing \eqref{eq:jd_case_i} by \eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_i} every constant
cancels except the Gamma rate and shape, giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:post_case_i}
  y \mid \mathrm{obs}
  \;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}(k I_0,\; k + R_0 F_r).
\end{equation}
The posterior rate $k + R_0 F_r > k$ reflects the fact that observing
no early transmission is evidence that $y$ is small: the index cohort
is likely a low-infectivity one.

\paragraph{Step 5: residual extinction probability and PMO.}
The conditional remaining-offspring rate is
$R_0 \Lambda = R_0(1 - F_r) y$, so the conditional extinction
probability is $q_r \mid y = e^{-R_0(1-q)(1-F_r) y}$.  Marginalising
over the posterior \eqref{eq:post_case_i} via the Gamma MGF,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qr_case_i}
  q_r
  \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-R_0(1-q)(1-F_r)y}\right]
  \;=\; \left(1 + \frac{R_0(1 - F_r)(1 - q)}{k + R_0 F_r}\right)^{-k I_0},
\end{equation}
and $\mathrm{PMO} = 1 - q_r$.

\paragraph{Closed-form SSE counterpart.}
Under the SSE model, $I_t \sim \mathrm{NB}(R_0 I_0 w_t,\, k I_0 w_t)$
independently across $t = 1, \ldots, r$ when $I_1 = \cdots = I_r = 0$,
so the general likelihood \eqref{eq:L_sse_general} reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_sse_case_i}
  L_\mathrm{SSE}
  \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^r \left(\frac{k}{k + R_0}\right)^{k I_0 w_t}
  \;=\; \left(1 + \frac{R_0}{k}\right)^{-k I_0 F_r}.
\end{equation}
The two likelihoods share the same functional shape but with different
exponents: the SSE exponent $k I_0 F_r$ is reduced by $F_r \leq 1$
relative to the SSI exponent $k I_0$, reflecting the fact that the SSE
total over days $1, \ldots, r$ has dispersion $k I_0 F_r$ whereas the
SSI total has dispersion $k I_0$.  Combined with the SSE PMO
$1 - q^{I_0(1 - F_r)}$ from \S~SSE model and the SSI PMO
\eqref{eq:qr_case_i}, the model-averaged PMO is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pmo_avg_case_i}
  \mathrm{PMO}_\mathrm{avg}
  \;=\; \pi_\mathrm{SSE}^\star\,\bigl(1 - q^{I_0(1 - F_r)}\bigr)
  + \pi_\mathrm{SSI}^\star\,\left[
    1 - \left(1 + \frac{R_0(1 - F_r)(1 - q)}{k + R_0 F_r}\right)^{-k I_0}
  \right].
\end{equation}
When $r = 0$, both likelihoods reduce to $1$, the posterior reduces to
the prior, and the model-averaged PMO is just the prior-weighted
average of the two per-model PMOs.

\begin{remark}[Consistency with the MCMC formula]
  When $I_1 = \cdots = I_r = 0$, the observations force
  $Y_1 = \cdots = Y_r = 0$, so $\Lambda = Y_0(1 - F_r)$ and the
  general MCMC posterior of \S~SSI model collapses to
  \eqref{eq:post_case_i}.  Taking the expectation of
  $e^{-R_0(1-q)(1-F_r)Y_0}$ with respect to that posterior via the
  Gamma MGF recovers \eqref{eq:qr_case_i}.
\end{remark}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Case (ii): \(I_0, I_i \geq 1\) for one later day \(i\),
all others zero}
\label{sec:ssi_case_ii}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

We now extend to $I_0 \geq 1$, $I_i \geq 1$ for some
$i \in \{1, \ldots, r\}$, and $I_d = 0$ for
$d \in \{1, \ldots, r\} \setminus \{i\}$.  Only $Y_0$ and $Y_i$ are
non-degenerate.

\paragraph{Step 1: conditional likelihood.}
Given $(Y_0, Y_i)$, the day-$d$ Poisson rate is $R_0 w_d Y_0$ for
$d \in \{1, \ldots, i\}$ and $R_0(w_d Y_0 + w_{d-i} Y_i)$ for
$d \in \{i+1, \ldots, r\}$.  Multiplying the day-$d$ Poisson pmfs,
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{P}(\mathrm{obs} \mid Y_0, Y_i)
  &= \underbrace{\prod_{d=1}^{i-1} e^{-R_0 w_d Y_0}}_{=\, e^{-R_0 F_{i-1} Y_0}}
  \;\cdot\;
  \underbrace{\frac{(R_0 w_i Y_0)^{I_i}}{I_i!}\, e^{-R_0 w_i
  Y_0}}_{\text{day-}i\text{ pmf}}
  \;\cdot\;
  \underbrace{\prod_{d=i+1}^{r} e^{-R_0(w_d Y_0 + w_{d-i} Y_i)}}_{=\,
  e^{-R_0(F_r - F_i) Y_0}\, e^{-R_0 F_{r-i} Y_i}}.
\end{align*}
The $Y_0$ exponentials collapse via $F_{i-1} + w_i + (F_r - F_i) = F_r$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cl_case_ii}
  \mathbb{P}(\mathrm{obs} \mid Y_0, Y_i)
  \;=\; \frac{(R_0 w_i Y_0)^{I_i}}{I_i!}\,
  e^{-R_0 F_r Y_0}\, e^{-R_0 F_{r-i} Y_i}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 2: joint density.}
Multiplying \eqref{eq:cl_case_ii} by the priors
$Y_0 \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(k I_0, k)$ and $Y_i \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(k I_i, k)$
(independent),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jd_case_ii}
  \begin{aligned}
    p(Y_0, Y_i, \mathrm{obs})
    &= \underbrace{\frac{(R_0 w_i)^{I_i}}{I_i!} \cdot
    \frac{k^{k I_0 + k I_i}}{\Gamma(k I_0)\, \Gamma(k I_i)}}_{\text{constants}}
    \\[2pt]
    &\quad\times\;
    \underbrace{Y_0^{k I_0 + I_i - 1}\, e^{-(k + R_0 F_r)
    Y_0}}_{\text{Gamma kernel in } Y_0}
    \;\cdot\;
    \underbrace{Y_i^{k I_i - 1}\, e^{-(k + R_0 F_{r-i})
    Y_i}}_{\text{Gamma kernel in } Y_i}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 3: likelihood.}
Integrating each Gamma kernel of \eqref{eq:jd_case_ii} via the Gamma
normalisation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_ssi_case_ii}
  L_\mathrm{SSI}^{(\mathrm{ii})}
  \;=\; \frac{(R_0 w_i)^{I_i}}{I_i!}\,
  \frac{\Gamma(k I_0 + I_i)}{\Gamma(k I_0)}\,
  \frac{k^{k I_0}}{(k + R_0 F_r)^{k I_0 + I_i}}\,
  \left(\frac{k}{k + R_0 F_{r-i}}\right)^{k I_i}.
\end{equation}
Setting $I_i = 0$ collapses the $\Gamma$-ratio and the second
parenthesis to $1$, recovering \eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_i}.

\paragraph{Step 4: posterior.}
Dividing \eqref{eq:jd_case_ii} by \eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_ii}, every
constant cancels and the two Gamma kernels normalise independently:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:post_case_ii}
  Y_0 \mid \mathrm{obs} \;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}(k I_0 + I_i,\; k + R_0 F_r),
  \qquad
  Y_i \mid \mathrm{obs} \;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}(k I_i,\; k + R_0 F_{r-i}).
\end{equation}
The shape of $Y_0$ is enriched by $I_i$: each observed offspring of
the index cohort acts as a Bayesian data point tilting $Y_0$ toward
higher values.  The $Y_i$ posterior is the day-0-only update
\eqref{eq:post_case_i} applied to the day-$i$ cohort over its residual
horizon $r - i$; setting $i = r$ gives $F_{r-i} = F_0 = 0$, so $Y_r$
collapses to its prior, since the day-$r$ cohort has not had a
chance to contribute.

\paragraph{Step 5: residual extinction probability and PMO.}
The remaining-offspring rate is $R_0 \Lambda$ with
$\Lambda = Y_0(1 - F_r) + Y_i(1 - F_{r-i})$, so the conditional
extinction probability is
$q_r \mid (Y_0, Y_i) = e^{-R_0(1-q)\Lambda}$.  Marginalising over the
two independent Gamma factors of \eqref{eq:post_case_ii} via the Gamma
MGF gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qr_case_ii}
  q_r
  \;=\; \left(1 + \frac{R_0(1 - F_r)(1 - q)}{k + R_0 F_r}\right)^{-(k
  I_0 + I_i)}
  \left(1 + \frac{R_0(1 - F_{r-i})(1 - q)}{k + R_0 F_{r-i}}\right)^{-k I_i},
\end{equation}
and $\mathrm{PMO} = 1 - q_r$.

\begin{remark}[Reduction to case~(i)]
  Setting $I_i = 0$ in \eqref{eq:qr_case_ii} collapses the second
  factor to $1$ and the first factor's exponent to $-k I_0$,
  recovering \eqref{eq:qr_case_i}; likewise \eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_ii}
  reduces to \eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_i}.  As the observation horizon
  extends ($r \to \infty$ with $i$ fixed), both factors of
  \eqref{eq:qr_case_ii} tend to $1$ and $q_r \to 1$.
\end{remark}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Case (iii): \(I_0, I_i, I_j \geq 1\) for two later days
\(i < j\), all others zero}
\label{sec:ssi_case_iii}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Finally, take $I_0 \geq 1$, $I_i \geq 1$, $I_j \geq 1$ for some
$1 \leq i < j \leq r$, and $I_d = 0$ for
$d \in \{1, \ldots, r\} \setminus \{i, j\}$.  Now only
$Y_0, Y_i, Y_j$ are non-degenerate.

\paragraph{Step 1: conditional likelihood.}
The conditional likelihood acquires an extra factor for the day-$j$
Poisson pmf, whose rate $R_0(w_j Y_0 + w_{j-i} Y_i)$ couples $Y_0$
and $Y_i$.  Repeating the telescoping argument of case~(ii) for each
latent separately,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cl_case_iii}
  \mathbb{P}(\mathrm{obs} \mid Y_0, Y_i, Y_j)
  \;=\; \frac{(R_0 w_i Y_0)^{I_i}}{I_i!}\,
  \frac{\bigl(R_0(w_j Y_0 + w_{j-i} Y_i)\bigr)^{I_j}}{I_j!}\,
  e^{-R_0 F_r Y_0}\, e^{-R_0 F_{r-i} Y_i}\, e^{-R_0 F_{r-j} Y_j}.
\end{equation}
Expanding the day-$j$ rate via the binomial theorem,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bin_case_iii}
  \bigl(w_j Y_0 + w_{j-i} Y_i\bigr)^{I_j}
  \;=\; \sum_{m=0}^{I_j} \binom{I_j}{m}\, w_j^{I_j - m}\, w_{j-i}^{m}\,
  Y_0^{I_j - m}\, Y_i^{m}.
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}[Latent-partition interpretation]
  Equivalently, by Poisson superposition we may regard $I_j$ as the
  sum of two independent Poissons --- one with mean $R_0 w_j Y_0$
  counting day-$j$ infections that originated from the day-$0$
  cohort, and one with mean $R_0 w_{j-i} Y_i$ counting those from the
  day-$i$ cohort.  Each term $m$ in \eqref{eq:bin_case_iii} is the
  joint probability that exactly $I_j - m$ originate from $Y_0$ and
  $m$ from $Y_i$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Step 2: joint density.}
Multiplying \eqref{eq:cl_case_iii} by the three independent priors
$\mathrm{Gamma}(k I_0, k)$, $\mathrm{Gamma}(k I_i, k)$,
$\mathrm{Gamma}(k I_j, k)$ and inserting \eqref{eq:bin_case_iii},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jd_case_iii}
  \begin{aligned}
    p(Y_0, Y_i, Y_j, \mathrm{obs})
    &= \frac{(R_0 w_i)^{I_i}\, R_0^{I_j}}{I_i!\, I_j!}\,
    \frac{k^{k I_0 + k I_i + k I_j}}{\Gamma(k I_0)\, \Gamma(k I_i)\,
    \Gamma(k I_j)}\,
    Y_j^{k I_j - 1}\, e^{-(k + R_0 F_{r-j}) Y_j}\\[2pt]
    &\quad\times\;\sum_{m=0}^{I_j} \binom{I_j}{m}\, w_j^{I_j - m}\,
    w_{j-i}^{m}\,
    Y_0^{k I_0 + I_i + I_j - m - 1}\, e^{-(k + R_0 F_r) Y_0}\,
    Y_i^{k I_i + m - 1}\, e^{-(k + R_0 F_{r-i}) Y_i}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This is a constant times a Gamma kernel in $Y_j$ (the same in every
mixture component) times a finite mixture (over
$m \in \{0, \ldots, I_j\}$) of Gamma-product kernels in $(Y_0, Y_i)$.

\paragraph{Step 3: likelihood.}
Integrating \eqref{eq:jd_case_iii} via the Gamma normalisation in each
variable, define the per-mixture-component constants
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cm_case_iii}
  c_m \;:=\; \binom{I_j}{m}\, w_j^{I_j - m}\, w_{j-i}^{m}\,
  \frac{\Gamma(k I_0 + I_i + I_j - m)}{(k + R_0 F_r)^{k I_0 + I_i + I_j - m}}\,
  \frac{\Gamma(k I_i + m)}{(k + R_0 F_{r-i})^{k I_i + m}}.
\end{equation}
Cancelling the $\Gamma(k I_j)$ factors and grouping $k^{k I_j}$ with
the $Y_j$-rate gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_ssi_case_iii}
  L_\mathrm{SSI}^{(\mathrm{iii})}
  \;=\; \frac{(R_0 w_i)^{I_i}\, R_0^{I_j}}{I_i!\, I_j!}\,
  \frac{k^{k I_0 + k I_i}}{\Gamma(k I_0)\, \Gamma(k I_i)}\,
  \left(\frac{k}{k + R_0 F_{r-j}}\right)^{k I_j}
  \sum_{m=0}^{I_j} c_m.
\end{equation}
Setting $I_j = 0$ collapses the sum to its $m = 0$ term and the
$(k/(k + R_0 F_{r-j}))^{k I_j}$ factor to $1$, recovering
\eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_ii}.

\paragraph{Step 4: posterior.}
Dividing \eqref{eq:jd_case_iii} by \eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_iii}: the
$Y_j$-Gamma rate-and-shape pair appears in both numerator and
denominator and gives the
$\mathrm{Gamma}(Y_j \mid k I_j, k + R_0 F_{r-j})$ density.  Within the
sum, the constants outside cancel; what remains in the $m$th term is
$c_m$ (numerator) divided by $\sum_{m'} c_{m'}$ (denominator), times
the two unnormalised $(Y_0, Y_i)$ Gamma kernels each with their proper
normalising constants supplied directly from the $c_m$ in the
denominator.  The result is a finite mixture of independent Gamma
products,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:post_case_iii}
  \begin{aligned}
    &p(Y_0, Y_i, Y_j \mid \mathrm{obs})
    \;=\; \mathrm{Gamma}\bigl(Y_j \,\big|\, k I_j,\; k + R_0 F_{r-j}\bigr)
    \\[2pt]
    &\qquad\times\;\sum_{m=0}^{I_j} \rho_m\,
    \mathrm{Gamma}\bigl(Y_0 \,\big|\, k I_0 + I_i + I_j - m,\; k +
    R_0 F_r\bigr)\,
    \mathrm{Gamma}\bigl(Y_i \,\big|\, k I_i + m,\; k + R_0 F_{r-i}\bigr),
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with normalised mixture weights
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_case_iii}
  \rho_m \;=\; \frac{c_m}{\sum_{m' = 0}^{I_j} c_{m'}}.
\end{equation}
The mixture index $m$ tracks how many of the $I_j$ day-$j$ infections
are attributed to $Y_i$ versus $Y_0$; the weights $\rho_m$ encode
which partition is most consistent with the prior infectivity
distributions, the serial-interval weights $w_j$, $w_{j-i}$, and the
dispersion $k$.

\paragraph{Step 5: residual extinction probability and PMO.}
The remaining-offspring rate is now
$R_0 \Lambda = R_0 [Y_0(1 - F_r) + Y_i(1 - F_{r-i}) + Y_j(1 - F_{r-j})]$,
so the conditional extinction probability is
$q_r \mid (Y_0, Y_i, Y_j) = e^{-R_0(1-q)\Lambda}$.  Marginalising
over the posterior \eqref{eq:post_case_iii}, $Y_j$'s expectation
factors out (its density is independent of $m$); within each mixture
component the $(Y_0, Y_i)$-densities are independent Gammas whose
MGFs evaluate term-by-term, giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qr_case_iii}
  q_r \;=\; \beta_j^{-k I_j} \sum_{m=0}^{I_j} \rho_m\,
  \beta_0^{-(k I_0 + I_i + I_j - m)}\, \beta_i^{-(k I_i + m)},
\end{equation}
where, for $a \in \{0, i, j\}$ (with the convention $F_{r-0} = F_r$),
\[
  \beta_a \;=\; 1 + \frac{R_0(1 - q)(1 - F_{r-a})}{k + R_0 F_{r-a}}.
\]

\begin{remark}[Reductions to simpler cases]
  When $I_j \to 0$ the sum collapses to its $m = 0$ term
  ($\rho_0 = 1$) and $\beta_j^{-k I_j} \to 1$: \eqref{eq:qr_case_iii}
  reduces to \eqref{eq:qr_case_ii}.  When also $I_i \to 0$, the
  exponent on $\beta_i$ in \eqref{eq:qr_case_ii} vanishes and the
  exponent on $\beta_0$ reduces from $-(k I_0 + I_i)$ to $-k I_0$,
  recovering \eqref{eq:qr_case_i}.  Similarly
  \eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_iii} reduces to
  \eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_ii} and then to \eqref{eq:L_ssi_case_i}.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Computational note.}
The $c_m$ involve $\Gamma$ factors of size up to
$k I_0 + I_i + I_j$, so for moderate $I_j$ a log-domain computation
($\log\Gamma$, $\mathrm{logsumexp}$) is recommended.  The number of
sum terms is $I_j + 1$, typically small for the histories of interest.

% ===========================================================================
\section{MCMC-based estimators of the SSI marginal likelihood}
\label{sec:mcmc_evidence}
% ===========================================================================

We describe three estimators of the integral
\eqref{eq:L_ssi_integral}.  Writing
$\pi(Y) = \prod_{t \in \mathcal T} p_Y(Y_t \mid I_t)$
for the integrand's prior factor restricted to the active days
$\mathcal{T} = \{t \in \{0,\ldots,r-1\} : I_t > 0\}$ (a product of
  independent Gammas, treating the observed cohort sizes $I_t$ as fixed
shape parameters), and
$\mathcal L(Y) = \prod_{t=1}^{r} p_I(I_t \mid Y_{0:t-1})$
for the integrand's likelihood factor, the target is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_def}
  L_\mathrm{SSI}
  \;=\; \int \mathcal L(Y)\, \pi(Y)\, \mathrm{d}Y
  \;=\; \mathbb E_{Y \sim \pi}\!\bigl[\mathcal L(Y)\bigr].
\end{equation}
The unnormalised posterior (the joint integrand) is
$\tilde p(Y) = \mathcal L(Y)\, \pi(Y)$, and the posterior density of the
latents given the observations is
$p_\mathrm{post}(Y) = \tilde p(Y) / L_\mathrm{SSI}$.  The MCMC sampler of
\S~\ref{sec:qr_ssi} produces draws $Y^{p,(j)} \sim p_\mathrm{post}$ for
$j = 1, \ldots, N_1$.

\subsection{Naive Monte Carlo}
\label{sec:naive_mc_evidence}

Equation~\eqref{eq:L_def} is itself an expectation under the prior $\pi$,
so the simplest estimator just averages the conditional likelihood at
independent draws from $\pi$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_ssi_naive_mc}
  \widehat L_\mathrm{SSI}^{\,\mathrm{naive}}
  \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathcal L\!\bigl(Y^{(n)}\bigr),
  \qquad Y^{(n)} \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \pi.
\end{equation}
No MCMC trace is needed: drawing $Y^{(n)}$ requires only independent
Gamma$(kI_t,k)$ samples for each $t \in \mathcal T$.  In log space the
implementation is
\begin{equation*}
  \log \widehat L_\mathrm{SSI}^{\,\mathrm{naive}}
  \;=\; \operatornamewithlimits{logsumexp}_{n=1,\ldots,N}\!\log
  \mathcal L(Y^{(n)}) \;-\; \log N.
\end{equation*}

The estimator is unbiased.  Its relative variance is
$\mathrm{Var}_\pi[\mathcal L]/(N L_\mathrm{SSI}^2)$, equivalently
$\bigl(\chi^2(p_\mathrm{post} \,\|\, \pi) + 1\bigr)/N - 1/N$, where
$\chi^2(p \,\|\, q) = \int (p/q - 1)^2 q$ is the $\chi^2$ divergence.  The
$\chi^2$ between prior and posterior is small whenever the data are
weakly informative about the latents — and remarkably, in the SSI model
this remains true even on moderately informative histories, because the
prior variance $I_t/k$ scales automatically with the cohort size $I_t$
and so retains overlap with the posterior.  The estimator becomes
unreliable only when the data are very informative (e.g.\ long histories
with substantial incidence at small $k$).

\subsection{Importance sampling}
\label{sec:is_evidence}

Importance sampling generalises the naive estimator by sampling from a
proposal $g$ chosen to better cover the posterior:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_ssi_is}
  \widehat L_\mathrm{SSI}^{\,\mathrm{IS}}
  \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N}
  \frac{\tilde p\!\bigl(Y^{(n)}\bigr)}{g\!\bigl(Y^{(n)}\bigr)},
  \qquad Y^{(n)} \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} g.
\end{equation}
The estimator is unbiased for any proposal $g$ with $g(Y) > 0$ wherever
$\tilde p(Y) > 0$; its relative variance is
$\chi^2(p_\mathrm{post} \,\|\, g)/N$, minimised by taking $g$ as close to
$p_\mathrm{post}$ as possible.

We take
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma_proposal}
  g(Y) \;=\; \prod_{t \in \mathcal T} \mathrm{Gamma}(Y_t;\, \alpha_t, \beta_t),
\end{equation}
i.e.\ a product of independent Gammas in the same family as the prior,
with shape and rate parameters fitted by method of moments to the
per-day marginals of the MCMC posterior trace
$\{Y^{p,(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N_1}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mom_fit}
  \alpha_t \;=\; \frac{\bar Y_t^{\,2}}{S_t^2},
  \qquad
  \beta_t  \;=\; \frac{\bar Y_t}{S_t^2},
  \qquad t \in \mathcal T,
\end{equation}
where $\bar Y_t$ and $S_t^2$ are the sample mean and (unbiased) sample
variance of $\{Y_t^{p,(j)}\}_j$.  Matching the proposal family to the
prior keeps importance weights bounded; matching its marginals to the
MCMC posterior makes it close to $p_\mathrm{post}$ marginally.

\begin{remark}[Caveat: posterior correlations]
  Equation~\eqref{eq:gamma_proposal} is a product proposal, while the
  joint posterior of $Y_\mathcal T$ may have non-trivial correlations
  arising from the shared force-of-infection terms.  When such
  correlations are strong, $\chi^2(p_\mathrm{post} \,\|\, g)$ does not
  shrink to zero in the large-data limit and the IS estimator's variance
  remains bounded below by that mismatch.  Bridge sampling
  (\S~\ref{sec:bridge_evidence}) is more forgiving of proposal
  mismatch.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Bridge sampling}
\label{sec:bridge_evidence}

Bridge sampling uses draws from \emph{both} the proposal $g$ and the
posterior $p_\mathrm{post}$, weighted by a bridge function $\alpha$.
The starting identity (Meng \& Wong, 1996) is the algebraic equality
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bridge_identity}
  L_\mathrm{SSI}
  \;=\;
  \frac{\mathbb E_{Y \sim g}\!\bigl[\tilde p(Y)\, \alpha(Y)\bigr]}
  {\mathbb E_{Y \sim p_\mathrm{post}}\!\bigl[g(Y)\, \alpha(Y)\bigr]},
\end{equation}
valid for any $\alpha$ such that
$0 < \int g(Y) \tilde p(Y) \alpha(Y)\, \mathrm{d}Y < \infty$.  The
identity is verified by writing the numerator as
$\int \tilde p\, g\, \alpha\, \mathrm{d}Y$ and the denominator as
$L_\mathrm{SSI}^{-1} \int \tilde p\, g\, \alpha\, \mathrm{d}Y$.  Replacing
the expectations by Monte Carlo averages over
$\{Y^{q,(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_2} \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} g$ and
$\{Y^{p,(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N_1} \sim p_\mathrm{post}$ (the MCMC trace) gives
the bridge-sampling estimator.

The asymptotic variance of $\widehat L_\mathrm{SSI}$ depends on the
choice of $\alpha$.  Minimising it (Meng \& Wong, 1996) yields the
optimal bridge
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alpha_star}
  \alpha^*(Y)
  \;=\;
  \frac{C}{s_1\, g(Y) + s_2\, \tilde p(Y) / L_\mathrm{SSI}},
\end{equation}
where $s_1 = N_1/(N_1 + N_2)$, $s_2 = N_2/(N_1 + N_2)$, and $C$ is any
non-zero constant (any rescaling of $\alpha$ cancels between numerator
and denominator of \eqref{eq:bridge_identity}).  The optimal bridge
depends on the unknown $L_\mathrm{SSI}$; substituting
\eqref{eq:alpha_star} into \eqref{eq:bridge_identity} and replacing the
expectations by Monte Carlo averages produces the fixed-point identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_ssi_bridge}
  L_\mathrm{SSI}
  \;=\;
  \frac{\displaystyle \frac{1}{N_2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_2}
    \frac{\tilde p(Y^{q,(i)})}{s_1\, g(Y^{q,(i)}) + s_2\, \tilde
  p(Y^{q,(i)})/L_\mathrm{SSI}}}
  {\displaystyle \frac{1}{N_1}\sum_{j=1}^{N_1}
    \frac{g(Y^{p,(j)})}{s_1\, g(Y^{p,(j)}) + s_2\, \tilde
  p(Y^{p,(j)})/L_\mathrm{SSI}}}.
\end{equation}
Solving \eqref{eq:L_ssi_bridge} for $L_\mathrm{SSI}$ requires iterating
the right-hand side from an initial guess.  Bridge sampling has lower
(or equal) asymptotic variance than importance sampling with the same
proposal, and the variance bound is governed by a symmetrised
divergence rather than $\chi^2(p_\mathrm{post} \,\|\, g)$, so it is
significantly more robust to proposal--posterior mismatch.

In practice we work entirely in log space for numerical stability.
Define the log importance ratios at the two sample sets,
\begin{equation*}
  \ell_q^{(i)} \;=\; \log \tilde p(Y^{q,(i)}) - \log g(Y^{q,(i)}),
  \qquad
  \ell_p^{(j)} \;=\; \log \tilde p(Y^{p,(j)}) - \log g(Y^{p,(j)}).
\end{equation*}
Multiplying numerator and denominator of \eqref{eq:L_ssi_bridge} by
$1/g(Y^{q,(i)})$ and $1/g(Y^{p,(j)})$ respectively and taking logs gives
the log-space iteration
\begin{multline}\label{eq:L_ssi_bridge_logspace}
  \log L^{(t+1)} \;=\;
  \operatornamewithlimits{logsumexp}_{i=1,\ldots,N_2}
  \!\Bigl[\,\ell_q^{(i)} -
    \mathrm{lse}\bigl(\log s_1,\, \log s_2 + \ell_q^{(i)} - \log
  L^{(t)}\bigr)\Bigr]
  - \log N_2 \\
  \;-\;
  \operatornamewithlimits{logsumexp}_{j=1,\ldots,N_1}
  \!\Bigl[\,-\mathrm{lse}\bigl(\log s_1,\, \log s_2 + \ell_p^{(j)} -
  \log L^{(t)}\bigr)\Bigr]
  + \log N_1,
\end{multline}
where $\mathrm{lse}(a,b) = \log(e^a + e^b)$.  We use the same Gamma
proposal $g$ as in \S~\ref{sec:is_evidence}, initialise $\log L^{(0)}$
at the median of $\{\ell_q^{(i)}\}_i$, and iterate
\eqref{eq:L_ssi_bridge_logspace} until $\log L^{(t)}$ stops changing
appreciably.

\section{Uncertainty in the parameters \(R_0\) and \(k\)}
\label{sec:param_uncertain}
% ===========================================================================

The preceding sections have treated the offspring-distribution
parameters as known.  In practice they are estimated from the data; we
equip them with priors and ask for the resulting marginal model
evidence and probability of a major outbreak.  Write \(\theta\) for the
collection of parameters of model \(M\)
(\(\theta = (R_0, k)\) for SSE and SSI, \(\theta = R_0\) for Poisson),
and assume the prior factorises as a product of Gamma or LogNormal
factors on the individual components; a fixed parameter corresponds to
its prior factor being a point mass, which drops out of the integrals
below without affecting the structure of any estimator.

Conditional on \(\theta\), each model has a per-parameter evidence
\(L_M(\theta) = \mathbb{P}_M(I_1, \ldots, I_r \mid I_0, \theta)\) and
PMO \(\mathrm{PMO}_M(\theta) = 1 - q_r^M(\theta)\): the SSE evidence is
the closed-form negative-binomial product \eqref{eq:L_sse_general}, the
Poisson evidence is the closed-form Poisson product
\eqref{eq:L_poisson_general}, and the SSI evidence is the latent
integral \eqref{eq:L_ssi_integral}.  The per-parameter PMOs are the
parameter-known quantities of \S\S~\ref{sec:qr_sse}--\ref{sec:qr_poisson}.

\subsection{Marginal evidence and model-averaged PMO}

Define the prior-marginal evidence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_marginal}
  \bar L_M \;=\; \int L_M(\theta)\, \pi(\theta)\, \mathrm{d}\theta,
\end{equation}
and the corresponding marginal probability of a major outbreak,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pmo_marginal}
  \overline{\mathrm{PMO}}_M
  \;=\; \int \mathrm{PMO}_M(\theta)\,
        p(\theta \mid I_{1:r}, M)\, \mathrm{d}\theta
  \;=\; \frac{1}{\bar L_M}
        \int \mathrm{PMO}_M(\theta)\, L_M(\theta)\, \pi(\theta)\,
              \mathrm{d}\theta,
\end{equation}
the posterior expectation of \(\mathrm{PMO}_M(\theta)\) under the
parameter posterior of model \(M\).  In the limit of a degenerate
prior at \(\theta_0\), \eqref{eq:L_marginal} reduces to
\(L_M(\theta_0)\) and \eqref{eq:pmo_marginal} reduces to
\(\mathrm{PMO}_M(\theta_0)\), recovering the parameter-known
quantities of the preceding sections.

The Bayesian model-averaging formulae \eqref{eq:pi_post_general} and
\eqref{eq:pmo_avg_general} of \S~\ref{sec:pmo_uncertain} carry over
unchanged with \(\bar L_M\) and \(\overline{\mathrm{PMO}}_M\) in place
of their parameter-known counterparts: the posterior model
probabilities are \(\pi_M^\star \propto \pi_M\, \bar L_M\) and the
model-averaged probability of a major outbreak is
\(\overline{\mathrm{PMO}}_\mathrm{avg}
  = \sum_{M \in \mathcal{M}} \pi_M^\star\,
    \overline{\mathrm{PMO}}_M\).  The two-model SSE/SSI case of
\eqref{eq:pmo_avg_two_model} is the worked specialisation.

\subsection{Estimators on the augmented latent vector}
\label{sec:param_uncertain_estimators}

The three estimators of \S~\ref{sec:mcmc_evidence} carry over to
\eqref{eq:L_marginal} after augmenting the latent vector with the
uncertain parameters.  Write
\(\theta = (\theta_{\mathrm u}, \theta_{\mathrm f})\) where
\(\theta_{\mathrm u}\) collects the components of \(\theta\) carrying a
non-degenerate prior factor and \(\theta_{\mathrm f}\) collects the
remaining (fixed) components.  The augmented latent vector is
\begin{equation*}
  z \;=\; (\theta_{\mathrm u},\, Y_{\mathcal T}),
\end{equation*}
where \(Y_{\mathcal T}\) is the SSI latent infectivities on the active
days \(\mathcal T = \{t \in \{0,\dots,r-1\} : I_t > 0\}\); for the SSE
and Poisson models this block is empty and \(z = \theta_{\mathrm u}\).
The unnormalised target on \(z\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:augmented_target}
  \tilde p(z)
  \;=\;
  \mathcal L\!\bigl(z;\, \theta_{\mathrm f}\bigr)\,
  \pi_Y\!\bigl(Y_{\mathcal T} \mid k\bigr)\,
  \pi(\theta_{\mathrm u}),
\end{equation}
where
\(\mathcal L(z; \theta_{\mathrm f}) =
   p(I_{1:r} \mid z, \theta_{\mathrm f})\) is the observation
likelihood — the SSE NB product \eqref{eq:L_sse_general} for SSE, the
Poisson product \eqref{eq:L_poisson_general} for Poisson, and the
latent-conditional Poisson product
\(\prod_{t=1}^r p_I(I_t \mid Y_{0:t-1})\) of \eqref{eq:joint_ssi} for
SSI — and \(\pi_Y\) is the SSI latent prior of \S~\ref{sec:mcmc_evidence}
(absent in the SSE and Poisson cases).  By construction
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lbar_normalising}
  \bar L_M \;=\; \int \tilde p(z)\, \mathrm{d}z,
\end{equation}
so \(\bar L_M\) is the normalising constant of the augmented target and
\(p_{\mathrm{post}}(z) = \tilde p(z) / \bar L_M\) is the augmented
posterior.  When \(\theta_{\mathrm u}\) is empty, \eqref{eq:augmented_target}
collapses to the latent-only target \(\tilde p(Y)\) of
\eqref{eq:L_def} and the development below reduces to that of
\S~\ref{sec:mcmc_evidence}.

\paragraph{Naive Monte Carlo.}  Draw
\(\theta_{\mathrm u}^{(n)} \sim \pi\) and, for the SSI model,
\(Y_{\mathcal T}^{(n)} \mid k^{(n)} \sim \pi_Y(\cdot \mid k^{(n)})\),
jointly forming \(z^{(n)}\).  The estimator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_naive_param}
  \widehat{\bar L}_M^{\,\mathrm{naive}}
  \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N
        \mathcal L\!\bigl(z^{(n)};\, \theta_{\mathrm f}\bigr)
\end{equation}
is unbiased.  For the SSE and Poisson models the latent block is empty
and \(\mathcal L(z; \theta_{\mathrm f})\) is the closed-form
\(L_M(\theta^{(n)})\) of \eqref{eq:L_sse_general} or
\eqref{eq:L_poisson_general}; no MCMC is required.  Naive MC inherits
the variance pathology of \S~\ref{sec:naive_mc_evidence}: it is reliable
when the prior and the augmented posterior overlap broadly, and degrades
on informative histories where the posterior concentrates in a region of
low prior mass.

\paragraph{Importance sampling.}  Run HMC against \(\tilde p\) of
\eqref{eq:augmented_target} to obtain posterior draws of the augmented
vector \(z\).  Fit an independent-Gamma proposal
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma_proposal_param}
  g(z) \;=\; \prod_j \mathrm{Gamma}\!\bigl(z_j;\, \alpha_j, \beta_j\bigr)
\end{equation}
by method of moments \eqref{eq:mom_fit} to each marginal of the trace
(the index \(j\) now ranges over the components of \(\theta_{\mathrm u}\)
as well as the active days \(\mathcal T\)).  The importance-sampling
estimator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_is_param}
  \widehat{\bar L}_M^{\,\mathrm{IS}}
  \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N
        \frac{\tilde p\!\bigl(z^{(n)}\bigr)}{g\!\bigl(z^{(n)}\bigr)},
  \qquad z^{(n)} \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} g,
\end{equation}
is the literal extension of \eqref{eq:L_ssi_is}; the variance
discussion of \S~\ref{sec:is_evidence} applies verbatim with
\(\chi^2(p_{\mathrm{post}} \,\|\, g)\) now measured on the augmented
space.  The Gamma proposal again matches the support of every
component of \(z\): the latent infectivities are Gamma a priori, and
both Gamma and LogNormal parameter priors are supported on the
positive reals.

\paragraph{Bridge sampling.}  With \(\{z^{p,(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N_1}\) from
the HMC trace and \(\{z^{q,(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_2}\) from the proposal
\eqref{eq:gamma_proposal_param}, define the augmented log importance
ratios
\begin{equation*}
  \ell_q^{(i)} \;=\; \log \tilde p\bigl(z^{q,(i)}\bigr) -
                     \log g\bigl(z^{q,(i)}\bigr),
  \qquad
  \ell_p^{(j)} \;=\; \log \tilde p\bigl(z^{p,(j)}\bigr) -
                     \log g\bigl(z^{p,(j)}\bigr).
\end{equation*}
The Meng--Wong identity \eqref{eq:bridge_identity} and its optimal
bridge \(\alpha^*\) are unchanged in form (with \(\bar L_M\) replacing
\(L_{\mathrm{SSI}}\) and the augmented variables in place of \(Y\)),
and the log-space fixed-point iteration
\eqref{eq:L_ssi_bridge_logspace} applies verbatim.

\subsection{Simulation-based rejection scheme}

The rejection sampler of \S~\ref{sec:simulation} extends without
modification: for each simulated trajectory under model \(M\), draw a
fresh \(\theta^{(i)} \sim \pi\), simulate the SSE / SSI / Poisson
generative process forward with parameters \(\theta^{(i)}\), and retain
only the trajectories whose first \(r\) cohorts match the observed
\(I_{1:r}\).  The acceptance probability of a single \(M\)-trajectory is
\(\int p(I_{1:r} \mid \theta, M)\, \pi(\theta)\, \mathrm{d}\theta =
  \bar L_M\), so the empirical fraction of accepted trajectories that
go on to a major outbreak is consistent for
\(\overline{\mathrm{PMO}}_M\) of \eqref{eq:pmo_marginal}.  Pooling
trajectories across the candidate set \(\mathcal{M}\) with per-batch
model split \(\mathrm{Multinomial}(b, (\pi_M)_{M \in \mathcal{M}})\)
realises the posterior model partition with expected fractions
\(\pi_M\,\bar L_M\), so the composition of the accepted pool delivers
the N-model average \(\overline{\mathrm{PMO}}_\mathrm{avg}\) of
\eqref{eq:pmo_marginal} together with
\eqref{eq:pi_post_general}--\eqref{eq:pmo_avg_general}, exactly as the
two-model SSE/SSI case of \S~\ref{sec:simulation}.
